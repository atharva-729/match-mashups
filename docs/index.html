How the excitement curve is measured ↓
That is the result. Everything below is the how.

Both featured videos were cut automatically from the full broadcast of one game. A program reads the on-screen scoreboard to know which play is showing, scores how much every moment of the game matters, matches that against the rises and falls of a piece of music, and lands every big play on a hit in the music. The story (which plays to show, and the captions) was written by hand, and the song was picked by ear from the program's shortlist.

Why two lengths. In 60 seconds every play is a peak and there is no room for the stretches in between, so nothing can build. The 120-second cut can let the music drop after a tie and climb again, and that rhythm of answer, wait, answer is what made this game. The 60-second cut keeps the moments; the 120-second cut keeps the tension.

The game

Panthers @ Bengals, 2014 season, week 6. Final: 37–37 after overtime, one of the rare ties in the NFL. Cincinnati led early. Two Andy Dalton interceptions handed Carolina the lead. Then every score in the fourth quarter was answered, each with less time left: 24–24, 31–31 after a 97-yard kickoff return, a Bengals field goal with two minutes to go, and a Panthers kick to tie it with five seconds left. In overtime each side kicked a field goal, and with two seconds left the Bengals missed a 36-yard kick wide right. Almost all of the tension comes from the clock running out.

How it works

1. Which play is on screen

The broadcast's scoreboard (quarter, clock, score, down) is read every half second and matched to the exact play in the NFL's public play-by-play data. No clip is tagged by hand.

2. How the game feels: a curve based on research

The first version measured tension: how close the game is. A tied game late on scored as high as it gets, the whole time it stayed tied. But viewers react to change. Economists call the two parts surprise (how far the chances of winning just moved) and suspense (how far they could move next) (Ely, Frankel and Kamenica, 2015). Minute-by-minute TV audiences for tennis and football rise with both, and surprise matters most (Wimbledon: two to five times as much). Fans' heart rate spikes on a goal and then settles. So the new curve treats every swing in win probability as a spike that fades, on top of a smaller suspense level. A tie is now a spike and then a held breath.

3. Choosing the music

Each piece of music gets an energy curve (loudness and how fast the sound changes), and every window of about 600 pieces is compared with the cut's own excitement curve, allowing for small shifts in timing (dynamic time warping). Matching the shape alone picked calm waltzes and horror cues that fit on paper but felt wrong. The final pick was made by ear from well-known, dramatic pieces: Vivaldi's Winter, which fits this cut better than In the Hall of the Mountain King did in the first version.

4. Landing on the hits

The strong accents in the music are detected, and each key play is moved so that its result (the ball crossing the line, the kick going through) lands exactly on one, with the biggest swings on the biggest hits. The footage in between is sped up or slowed slightly (0.86× to 1.29×) to arrive on time; no play is cut.

5. Telling it as a story

The captions read as one story, with the clock counting down through them. The 60-second cut:

  1. It starts as Cincinnati's day. Gio Bernard goes 89 yards. 14–7.
  2. Then Andy Dalton starts handing the game away.
  3. Carolina takes over. Cam Newton runs it in. 24–17.
  4. Twelve minutes left, and Cincinnati claws back. 24–24.
  5. Carolina pulls ahead again, 31–24. Under five minutes to save it...
  6. ...and Cincinnati answers in one breath. 31–31.
  7. Two minutes left. Nugent puts the Bengals up 34–31. It should be over.
  8. Five seconds left, down three. One kick to save Carolina. 34–34.
  9. Overtime. Down three, two minutes left, Gano answers again. 37–37.
  10. Two seconds left. One kick to win it...
  11. Wide right. After all that, nobody wins.

What was learned

Works

  • Finding every play in a full broadcast, automatically
  • Timing: plays land on the music within a frame
  • A research-based measure of how a game feels
  • A new cut in minutes once the story is written

Not yet

  • The gut reaction. The numbers match; the feeling doesn't always follow
  • Every change that made it land came from a human: the song, the story, what to leave out

Probably why

  • Suspense needs a side to root for, and a neutral viewer has none
  • The music replaces the crowd and the commentators, the strongest cues in sport
  • 60 seconds leaves no room to build

Tried and dropped: In the Hall of the Mountain King and Danse Macabre versions of the featured cut; tracks chosen by shape alone; slow motion and on-screen clock call-outs at the late plays (too distracting, so the clock went into the captions).
